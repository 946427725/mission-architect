# Mission Architect: one-lesson teacher guide

**Age:** 12-18 · **Time:** 45 minutes · **Group size:** teams of 2-4 on one computer · **Needs:** a browser, no install

## Learning goals
By the end, students can:
1. Explain why missions must launch in a **launch window** (planets have to line up).
2. Describe at least two **engineering trade-offs** (e.g. more instruments mean more mass, power and cost).
3. Explain why **margins** (spare power, fuel, budget) matter when things go wrong.
4. Compare their design with a real NASA mission and explain one difference.

## Lesson plan

| Minutes | Activity |
|---|---|
| 0-5 | Hook: "NASA sent a nuclear battery to Pluto. Why not solar panels?" Collect guesses. |
| 5-10 | Demo on the projector: Red Horizon → **Fly NASA's design** → press and hold Launch. Point out the launch-window chart and the gauges. |
| 10-30 | Teams play **Red Horizon** on **Easy** (or Normal for older students), in Team mode with the 4 roles. Each team must reach the science goal. |
| 30-38 | Teams read their report: "Why it turned out this way" and "One change that would have helped most". They apply one change and fly again. |
| 38-45 | Class discussion (prompts below). Compare scores on the leaderboard. |

## Team roles (Team mode)
- **Flight Dynamics Officer:** launch window and rocket. Goal: keep 10% spare rocket capacity.
- **Spacecraft Systems Engineer:** power, fuel, computers. Goal: 20% spare power and 10% spare fuel.
- **Principal Investigator:** instruments. Goal: 120% of the science target.
- **Ground & Budget Lead:** antenna, ground station, budget. Goal: 95% of data home and 10% budget left.

These goals conflict on purpose. Every change cancels everyone's sign-off, so teams have to negotiate.

## Discussion prompts

**Mars (Red Horizon)**
1. Opportunity was solar-powered and died in a 2018 dust storm. Perseverance carries a nuclear battery. What did your team choose, and why?
2. Why can't we launch to Mars any day we like? What happens every 26 months?
3. Your instruments make more data than the rover can send. Which would you drop, and why?

**Jupiter (Giant's Heart)**
1. Juno flew with giant solar panels 5 AU from the Sun. How much sunlight is left there?
2. Why did Juno need a titanium vault around its computer?
3. A shorter orbit gives more science but needs a bigger braking burn. Who on your team wanted which?

**Pluto (Edge of Light)**
1. Why is a direct flight to Pluto impossible with today's rockets, but a Jupiter flyby works?
2. New Horizons took 15 months to send its Pluto pictures home. What limits the data rate?
3. Why does every gram matter for the fastest probe ever launched?

## Extension
Use **Check the physics** on the Route step to see the real Perseverance and New Horizons launches on the chart. Ask: given only the launch and arrival dates, how close does the game get to NASA's real numbers? (Mars 2020 launch energy 14.4 vs ≈14.5; New Horizons 158.8 vs 157.7.)
